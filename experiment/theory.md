INTRODUCTION:

The clavicle, commonly known as the collarbone, is a slender, S-shaped long bone that lies horizontally at the base of the neck and forms an essential part of the shoulder (pectoral) girdle, connecting the upper limb to the trunk of the body. It acts as a structural strut between the sternum (breastbone) medially and the scapula (shoulder blade) laterally, enabling the shoulder to maintain its wide range of motion while remaining mechanically stable. The clavicle is the only long bone in the human body that lies horizontally, and each individual has one on the right and one on the left side of the body. Due to its subcutaneous position, the bone is easily felt beneath the skin and can often be visibly distinguished in lean individuals. Functionally, it serves multiple important roles: it transmits mechanical forces from the upper limb to the axial skeleton, protects vital neurovascular structures such as the brachial plexus and subclavian vessels that pass beneath it, and provides anchorage for various muscles involved in movements of the shoulder and neck. The medial end of the clavicle articulates with the sternum at the sternoclavicular joint, while the lateral end articulates with the acromion of the scapula at the acromioclavicular joint, both of which contribute to the clavicle’s ability to support and facilitate shoulder mobility. Developmentally, the clavicle is the first bone in the body to begin ossification in the embryo and continues to ossify into early adulthood, reflecting its complex embryological origin and structural significance. Clinically, the clavicle is one of the most frequently fractured bones due to its exposed location and role in absorbing impact forces, with midshaft fractures being the most common.

Overall, the clavicle’s structural design, functional contributions to shoulder mechanics, protective role for vital structures, and clinical significance make it a bone of major importance in both anatomy and musculoskeletal medicine.
